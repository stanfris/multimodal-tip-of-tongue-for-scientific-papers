Write a natural tip-of-the-tongue query of approximately 100-150 words using the memory cues below. The query should sound like an imperfect recollection, not a complete description of the document or a summary generated while viewing it.

Guidelines:

MUST FOLLOW:
1. Use only information supported by the memory cues. Do not invent claims about the paper's topic, method, results, datasets, or figures.
2. Choose one primary visual memory and at most two supporting visual
   details. Do not summarize the document's complete set of figures.
3. Omit generic properties that would rarely be remembered or help identify
   the paper, such as white backgrounds, black text, ordinary grid lines,
   column alignment, or the dimensions of conventional tables.
4. Organize the query around the primary visual memory. Integrate supporting
   details into the recollection rather than introducing each with repeated
   phrases such as "There was," "Another," or "Then there was."
5. Express uncertainty locally around particular remembered details, not only
   through a general statement that the title or authors cannot be recalled.
6. Prefer semantically or structurally distinctive figures over generic tables, formatting details, axes, grid lines, or ordinary document features.
7. Do not mention the paper's title, authors, affiliations, venue, DOI, citation information, or other direct identifiers, even if they occur in the cues.
8. Avoid copying long or unusually specific text labels verbatim when they could function as exact-match search keys. Describe their remembered meaning more generally unless the terminology is itself an essential memory cue.
9. Express the details with realistic uncertainty. The searcher may remember the broad appearance or relationship between elements without remembering every colour, label, position, or interpretation exactly.
10. Keep visual and textual recollections distinguishable. Do not claim that textual information appeared inside a figure unless the cues explicitly support this.
11. Write as a single coherent recollection rather than as a list of clues. Connect the most memorable details naturally.
12. Maintain a casual, conversational tone appropriate for someone genuinely requesting help online. Do not use headings, bullet points, or formal academic prose.
13. Begin directly with the recollection. Do not begin with a formal greeting or phrases such as "Hello" or "Hey everyone."
14. End with a natural request for help identifying the paper.
15. Output only the final query.

COULD FOLLOW:
1. Frequently convey uncertainty using varied and natural qualifications, such as questioning a colour, arrangement, interpretation, or relationship between remembered elements.
2. Often mention an unsuccessful earlier search, such as trying broad keywords, searching through references, or browsing papers with similar figures.
3. Often include a polite request for assistance or a brief expression of thanks.
4. Sometimes acknowledge being unable to remember a label, technical term, dataset, or part of a figure.
5. Sometimes mention a plausible general context in which the paper was encountered, such as during coursework, a literature review, thesis work, a seminar, or a research project.
6. Occasionally mention something the searcher believes the paper was not about or should not be confused with, but only when this can be stated without inventing unsupported facts.
7. Occasionally express mild frustration, curiosity, or another natural reaction to being unable to locate the paper.
8. You may introduce ambiguity between interpretations of a supplied cue, but do not fabricate concrete document details or deliberately contradict the cues.
9. Vary the organization and wording across generated queries. Do not repeatedly rely on stock openings such as "I remember encountering a paper a few years back" or stock endings such as "Does this sound familiar to anyone?"
