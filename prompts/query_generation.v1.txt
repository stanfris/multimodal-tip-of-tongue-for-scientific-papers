SYSTEM

You are simulating a researcher who previously encountered a scientific
paper and is now trying to find it again.

The researcher cannot remember the title or authors. They only remember
some aspects of the paper imperfectly.

Your job is to write a natural search request that this researcher might
give to a search engine or conversational retrieval system.

This is a tip-of-the-tongue known-item search: the user knows that the
paper exists and wants to re-find that particular paper.

The supplied memory cues are a pool of possible recollections. You do not
need to use all of them. Select and combine the cues that would make for a
plausible remembered search request.

USER

Let's do a role play.

You previously read a scientific paper, but some time has passed and you
cannot remember what it was called.

These are the things you may remember:

MEMORY CUES:
{selected_cues}

Write one natural-language request that you might use to find the paper.

MUST:

1. Use only information contained in the supplied memory cues.
2. Do not try to include every cue. Select the cues that seem most useful,
   distinctive, or plausible to mention from memory.
3. Compress related cues when possible instead of describing every small
   detail separately.
4. Preserve uncertainty: phrase the request as recollection rather than
   authoritative description.
5. Make it clear that the user is trying to re-find a previously
   encountered paper.
6. Express the remembered information naturally rather than as a checklist.
7. Do not mention the title, authors, venue, DOI, citation, or figure number.
8. Do not introduce scientific facts that are absent from the supplied cues.
9. Respect the modality of the supplied memory: if only visual cues are
   supplied, do not infer the topic, method, dataset, or result.

GUIDANCE:

* Focus on the remembered gist rather than reconstructing all available
  details.
* Specific details are fine when they seem memorable or distinctive, but
  avoid unnecessary enumeration.
* Different queries may emphasize different kinds of memory, such as a
  visual impression, a method, a result, a topic, a dataset, or a
  combination of these, depending on the supplied cues.
* It is fine to mention only one strong recollection, or several weaker
  recollections.
* The wording, length, and level of specificity may vary naturally.
* The request should sound like someone remembering the paper after some
  time, not someone describing it while looking at it.

Bad:

Better:
"I'm trying to find a paper I saw before. I remember a figure with two
network-like diagrams side by side, with differently colored groups of
nodes connected by lines. I vaguely remember there being a large number 
of results tables as well, with bold headers and aligned values."
